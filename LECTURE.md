# Disease-Specific Reporting: Why Nodal Nomenclature Matters

Nov 2, 2026 · @T Yo

That sums to 50 with no slack. If a case runs long, cut the thyroid aside in Case 2 first, then the Rotter's aside in Case 4. The posttest runs faster than the pretest because the images are familiar.

Pretest order: open the poll, let everyone vote, close it, then pick the hot seat and have them answer aloud with one sentence of reasoning. Say nothing beyond "noted" and move on. Your face does nothing during the pretest.

Quick-check order: pick the hot seat first, they answer aloud, then the room votes, then reveal. Anchoring is fine here; it is teaching, not measurement. Quick checks test the rule on a case not shown in the pretest, never the pretest item itself.

The pretest answers are revealed once, after the posttest, with the two bar sets side by side. No case restates a pretest question or puts the A/B labels back on a pretest image. The cases teach the boundary; the posttest measures whether the resident can carry it back to the test image.

## Opening (3 minutes, 2 slides)

**Slide: the thesis.** Before you describe a node, ask what disease is being staged, because that decides which words matter. Lymphoma reads nodes by region, so inside a region you can say "iliac" or "axillary" and stop; what lymphoma counts is how many regions and which side of the diaphragm. Carcinomas read nodes against a boundary that is different for every disease, so the same scan demands "common iliac" for prostate, "4R" for lung, "medial to pectoralis minor" for breast, and a lazy word quietly changes what the oncologist does. This talk is four of those boundaries. The staging letters are the reason the words matter; the words are the point.

**Slide: the four landmarks.** These are the four lines you will learn to name today, one per case. The common iliac bifurcation. The left lateral wall of the trachea. The dentate line and the mesorectal fascia. The pectoralis minor. Each one is a boundary where the same node changes its name and its consequence for at least one disease, and for lymphoma none of them matter at all.

Say out loud that the pretest answers are coming, that they were not expected to know them, and that the point of the next 40 minutes is to make the posttest feel obvious.

## Pretest (7 minutes, 4 questions)

Every question shows one PET/CT image with two avid nodes labeled A and B, names one disease, and asks the same thing. The option set is identical on every question so residents answer the question, not the format.

**Stem, every question:** "Does node A versus node B change management?"

**Options, every question:** (a) No, same management either way. (b) Yes, it changes what the surgeon does. (c) Yes, it changes what radiation covers. (d) Yes, it moves the patient from local treatment to systemic treatment.

| # | Image | Node A | Node B | Disease named | Correct | One-line reason (reveal only at posttest) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | PSMA PET/CT pelvis | External iliac | Common iliac, above the bifurcation | Prostate, newly diagnosed, no other disease | d | Below the bifurcation the node is pelvic and gets radiated with the prostate; above it the node is counted as metastatic and the patient is treated with hormones and drugs instead |
| 2 | FDG PET/CT chest, RUL mass | 4R | 4L | Lung adenocarcinoma, right upper lobe | d | Same side as the tumor, the surgeon may still operate; opposite side, surgery is off the table and the patient gets chemoradiation |
| 3 | FDG PET/CT pelvis | Internal iliac | Inguinal | Anal canal SCC | a | Both nodes count as regional for anal cancer and both are already inside the standard radiation field |
| 4 | FDG PET/CT axilla | Level II | Level III, medial to pectoralis minor | Breast cancer, ipsilateral | d | Level II goes to surgery; a node medial to pectoralis minor sends the patient to chemotherapy before any surgery |

Question 3 is the one with a "no" answer. Keep it; the posttest delta on that item tells you whether they learned the principle or just learned that the answer is always d.

For each question the hot seat, picked after the poll closes, gives their letter and one sentence. Do not confirm or correct. Nothing is revealed until after the posttest.

## Case 1: the common iliac node (8 minutes)

**The lazy sentence:** "PSMA-avid right iliac lymph node, 1.2 cm." Put it on the slide first. The urologist cannot tell from this whether the patient is a candidate for curative pelvic radiation or needs to start systemic therapy, so they call you or re-read the scan. Say this out loud the first time: the sentence is not wrong, it is incomplete for this disease. For lymphoma the same sentence would have been complete.

**The pair:** external iliac versus common iliac. Fresh PSMA PET/CT, draw the bifurcation.

**Lymphoma:** the iliac chain on one side is one region. Above or below the bifurcation changes nothing; the report can say "right iliac" and stop.

**Prostate:** the bifurcation is the edge of the pelvis. Below it, the node gets radiated with the prostate. Above it, the node counts as metastatic and the patient goes to hormone therapy and drugs instead. The report has to say which side of the bifurcation, with the vessel named.

**NCCN slide:** prostate guideline, N1 on the regional page and M1 on the metastatic page. "Same node, different page."

**Quick check, R1 hot seat:** verbal, same common iliac node, bladder cancer. "Does the bifurcation matter here?" No; for bladder that node is still regional and still in the plan. Same node, three answers: doesn't matter for lymphoma, decides everything for prostate, doesn't matter for bladder.

## Case 2: the paratracheal node (8 minutes)

**The lazy sentence:** "FDG-avid paratracheal lymph node, 1.5 cm." The thoracic surgeon cannot tell whether this patient is still theirs. Right or left is the whole referral, and the report did not say.

**The pair:** 4R versus 4L. Fresh FDG PET/CT chest, draw the left lateral wall of the trachea on the fused axial. Not the midline: the left wall.

**Lymphoma:** the whole mediastinum is one region. Right or left paratracheal changes nothing; what the lymphoma team wants from the chest is how big the mass is.

**Lung:** the side of the node relative to the tumor is the decision. Same side, the surgeon is still in the room and the patient may still get an operation after chemo-immunotherapy. Opposite side, surgery is off the table and the patient gets chemoradiation. A node just left of midline but medial to the left tracheal wall is still 4R, still same-side for a right lung tumor, so the report has to name the station, not describe the position.

**NCCN slide:** NSCLC stage III page, the same-side branch that still reaches surgery and the opposite-side branch that goes to chemoradiation.

**Quick check, R1 hot seat:** verbal, left lung tumor, avid subcarinal node. "Same side or opposite side?" Neither; subcarinal is a midline station and counts as same-side for every tumor, so surgery is still possible. The side rule is only for the paratracheal stations.

**Aside if time (cut first if behind):** the same superior mediastinal node is central-compartment disease for thyroid cancer and goes to the thyroid surgeon. One node, three services.

## Case 3: the inguinal node (8 minutes)

**The lazy sentence:** "FDG-avid pelvic and inguinal adenopathy." For an anal primary that is a complete report. For a rectal primary it is a stage IV diagnosis hidden in a phrase, and the colorectal surgeon has to go back to the images to find out which nodes you meant.

**The pair:** internal iliac versus inguinal. Fresh FDG PET/CT pelvis with a low primary. This case flips the pattern: here the node is the same and the primary moves.

**Lymphoma:** the pelvic nodes and the groin nodes are different regions, but a single node in either is the same stage and the same treatment. Which one it is does not change the plan.

**Anal cancer:** the groin is where anal cancer drains, so an inguinal node is part of the disease and already inside the radiation field. The report changes the dose, not the plan.

**Rectal cancer:** the same inguinal node means the cancer has left the pelvis. The patient is treated as metastatic and the curative surgery conversation changes. The dentate line, a centimeter of primary location, decides which of those two sentences the report supports.

**NCCN slide:** anal and rectal nodal definitions side by side, inguinal highlighted on each. No regimens; the two tables are the slide.

**Quick check, R1 hot seat:** verbal, anal cancer, avid external iliac node. "In the plan or out?" In; still regional, still in the field. "Same node, rectal cancer." Out; counted as metastatic. They can now place a node they were not shown.

**Size slide, optional:** for rectal cancer, pelvic sidewall nodes above about 7 mm short axis change whether the surgeon extends the dissection, so regional nodes need a measurement, not just a location.

## Case 4: the axilla (8 minutes)

**The lazy sentence:** "FDG-avid left axillary lymph node, 1.4 cm." The breast surgeon books a sentinel node procedure. If that node was medial to the pectoralis minor, the patient should have gone to medical oncology first, and the report is the reason they did not.

**Two pairs on one image.** Fresh FDG PET/CT axilla with the pectoralis minor drawn. Level I lateral to it, level II behind it, level III medial to it, Rotter's between the two pectoralis muscles.

**Pair one, Rotter's versus level II.**

Lymphoma: same region, same stage, same treatment. The report can say "axillary" and stop.

Breast: same stage too, but not the same operation. A standard axillary dissection does not reach the interpectoral space, so an unreported Rotter's node is a node left behind, and radiation planning covers it separately. The stage did not change; the surgeon's plan did.

**Pair two, level II versus level III.**

Lymphoma: different regions, but one region is one region. A single node at level II or at level III is the same stage and the same treatment, so II versus III does not change the plan.

Breast: II versus III is the whole decision. Level II goes to the surgeon. A node medial to the pectoralis minor sends the patient to the medical oncologist first, for chemotherapy before any operation, and the radiation plan expands. The report has to say which side of the muscle before the case is booked.

**Then the follow-up, II plus III.** Now both diseases care, for different reasons. Lymphoma: two regions instead of one, so the stage goes up. Breast: level III is involved, so it is the chemo-first pathway, and level II riding along adds nothing. Versus and plus are different questions, and the two diseases answer them in opposite order.

**NCCN slide:** breast workup page, the locally advanced branch routing to preoperative systemic therapy next to the operable branch.

**Quick check, R1 hot seat:** verbal, breast cancer, avid node, and you describe its position relative to the pectoralis minor without naming the level. "Which level, and does the patient see the surgeon or the oncologist first?" Run it twice with the node on each side of the muscle. The point is that they can translate position into the word the surgeon needs.

**Aside if time:** even lymphoma has more than one map. The Hodgkin trial groups count nodal areas differently from the standard regions, so the same axilla can be one area on one scheme and two on another. The counting rule belongs to the trial, not to anatomy.

## Close: the supraclavicular node (3 minutes)

**Slide: one node, seven labels.** One FDG-avid left supraclavicular node, primary withheld. Build the labels one at a time on the click.

| Primary | This node is | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lung | N3, even ipsilateral | Unresectable, definitive chemoradiation |
| Breast | cN3c, stage IIIC | Inoperable upfront, systemic therapy first |
| Thyroid | Lateral neck, N1b | Lateral neck dissection |
| Nasopharynx | N3 if below the caudal cricoid | Induction chemotherapy plus chemoradiation |
| Gastric or other GI (Virchow) | M1, stage IV | Palliative pathway |
| Cervix | FIGO IVB | Systemic |
| Lymphoma | Part of the cervical/supraclavicular region | One region, count the others |

Say it once: the node did not move. Only the rulebook did.

**Slide: three reporting rules.** Use the disease's own vocabulary: station for lung, level for breast and neck, region for lymphoma, named vessel for the pelvis. Give the short-axis size. Say explicitly which side of the boundary the node is on, and name the boundary. If the clinical history does not tell you the primary, say which classification you used, because the same sentence will be read by three different services.

## Posttest and wrap (5 minutes)

Same four questions, same images, same options, in the same order. No hot seat. Open each poll, close it after about 45 seconds, and move on without revealing until all four are in. Then reveal all four at once: pretest bars beside posttest bars, the correct letter marked, and the one-line reason from the pretest table. This is the only time the pretest answers appear.

Last slide is the thesis sentence, unchanged: ask what disease is being staged, because that decides which words matter. The residents have now seen it work four times, which is the number it takes to become a habit.
